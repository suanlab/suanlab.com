## 1. Spin is an internal degree of freedom

Spin describes how an internal quantum state transforms under rotations and which angular-momentum components can be measured. It is not a model of a tiny ball mechanically spinning around its own surface. For spin 1/2 the state space is C². We use a nonzero raw vector z and a normalized representative ψ=z/||z||, with the first and second standard basis vectors denoted e₀ and e₁.

These two entries are amplitudes, not two points in space. Nor does every physically encoded qubit implement spatial rotations in exactly the same way as a spin-1/2 particle. Here we explicitly choose the spin-1/2 representation. Preparation, active rotation and final spin measurement are distinct operations. Inspecting samples of a rotation does not insert measurements along one experimental trajectory.

## 2. Pauli matrices set the scale of spin

Choose a right-handed set of axes. The three spin components are Hermitian matrices proportional to the Pauli matrices. Their units are action, the same units as ℏ.

$$
\sigma_x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\sigma_y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
\sigma_z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad S_i=\frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_i.
$$

Each component has outcomes +ℏ/2 and −ℏ/2. The factor 1/2 is essential: treating σ itself as the physical spin would double every mean and quadruple every variance. The imaginary entries of σy also matter. Its adjoint uses conjugation as well as transposition.

An eigenstate of Sz can have a definite z result while x and y measurements each remain uncertain. This is a statement about outcomes on separately prepared copies. It does not assign simultaneous classical values to all three components.

## 3. A measurement direction selects two projectors

Let n be a real unit vector, and let r be the Bloch vector with components ψ†σiψ. For a pure C² state, r has length 1. Measuring along n uses the following projectors, probabilities and moments.

$$
S_{\mathbf n}=\frac{\hbar}{2}\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma,\quad
P_\pm=\frac{I\pm\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma}{2},\quad
p_\pm=\frac{1\pm\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r}{2},\qquad
\langle S_{\mathbf n}\rangle=\frac{\hbar}{2}\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r,\quad
\operatorname{Var}(S_{\mathbf n})=\frac{\hbar^2}{4}\left[1-(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r)^2\right].
$$

To see why, use the spectral identity Sn=(ℏ/2)P+−(ℏ/2)P−. The mean is the signed difference of the two probabilities, while the second moment is ℏ²/4 because both squared outcomes are equal. Subtracting the squared mean gives the variance. An expectation of 0 does not mean an individual reading is 0.

For e₀, r points along +z. A z measurement returns +ℏ/2 with certainty, whereas x and y give equal probabilities. For (e₀+e₁)/√2, r points along +x. Its z histogram is the same as that of (e₀−e₁)/√2, but the two preparations have opposite x results. One histogram cannot identify a ray.

## 4. Components and total squared spin are different

Pauli multiplication gives the angular-momentum commutator and fixes the total squared spin. Cyclic permutations give the other commutators.

$$
[S_x,S_y]=i\hbar S_z,\qquad
S_x^2=S_y^2=S_z^2=\frac{\hbar^2}{4}I,\qquad
\mathbf S^2=S_x^2+S_y^2+S_z^2=\frac{3\hbar^2}{4}I.
$$

The total squared spin has one eigenvalue, 3ℏ²/4, for this representation. Its square root is √3ℏ/2, while a component measurement has magnitude ℏ/2. These are different observables. A classical vector whose three components all have fixed simultaneous values is not a substitute for these noncommuting operators.

For e₀, the means are (0,0,ℏ/2). Their squared length is ℏ²/4. The missing ℏ²/2 in the sum of second moments comes from the x and y variances. More generally, a pure spin-1/2 state has squared mean-vector length ℏ²/4 even though the Casimir value remains 3ℏ²/4.

## 5. The rotation angle enters through a half-angle

An active rotation changes the state while the laboratory measurement axes remain fixed. A rotation by angle φ about unit axis a is generated by the spin component along a. Since (a·σ)²=I, its exponential separates into even and odd powers.

$$
U(\mathbf a,\phi)=\exp\left(-\frac{i\phi}{\hbar}\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf S\right)
=\cos\frac{\phi}{2}\,I-i\sin\frac{\phi}{2}\,\mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma,
\qquad \phi=2\pi\nu.
$$

The half-angle follows from S=ℏσ/2; it is not an arbitrary animation-speed choice. ℏ cancels from the geometric rotation matrix. Changing its declared numerical value rescales spin units but leaves the same angle's state transformation unchanged.

The parameter ν counts full spatial turns. It is not elapsed physical time. A Hamiltonian and its physical duration could generate a related rotation, but that requires an additional dynamical specification. Here a slider should be read as selecting a rotation angle. A Bloch camera drag only changes the viewing direction.

## 6. Calculate a positive X rotation by hand

Start with e₀ and rotate actively about +x. The two amplitudes and the resulting Bloch vector follow by multiplying the matrix in the preceding equation.

$$
\psi(\phi)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\phi/2)\\-i\sin(\phi/2)\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\mathbf r(\phi)=(0,-\sin\phi,\cos\phi),\qquad
p_Z(+)=\cos^2(\phi/2),\quad p_Y(+)=\frac{1-\sin\phi}{2}.
$$

At φ=π/2 the state is (1,−i)/√2 and points along −y on the Bloch sphere. The positive sign of the angle does not imply a positive y component: in a right-handed frame x×z=−y. At φ=π the state is −ie₁ and a z measurement gives the negative result with certainty.

| Spatial angle | State representative | Z positive probability | Mean Sz |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | e₀ | 1 | ℏ/2 |
| π/2 | (e₀−ie₁)/√2 | 1/2 | 0 |
| π | −ie₁ | 0 | −ℏ/2 |
| 2π | −e₀ | 1 | ℏ/2 |
| 4π | e₀ | 1 | ℏ/2 |

A useful comparison keeps the amplitude arrows, Bloch coordinates and probability bars linked. The complex representative carries more information than this one histogram. Conversely, the Bloch vector removes the representative's common phase.

## 7. Two spatial turns restore the spinor

The half-angle formula immediately gives a sign after one full spatial turn and restores the representative after two. For any normalized input, local spin probabilities remain unchanged by the first sign.

$$
U(\mathbf a,2\pi)=-I,\qquad U(\mathbf a,4\pi)=I,\qquad
|\langle\psi,-\psi\rangle|^2=1,\qquad
\|(-\psi)-\psi\|^2=4.
$$

The last two quantities answer different questions. Fidelity compares rays and ignores a common phase. Squared representative difference retains that phase convention. Neither should be silently substituted for the other in a plot or a numerical comparison.

SU(2) acts directly on spinors. Its two elements U and −U induce the same rotation of the Bloch vector in SO(3). This is the double-cover distinction. A return of the Bloch arrow after 2π does not mean the spinor representative has returned. A norm-preserving matrix alone also does not establish that it represents the intended rotation.

## 8. A common phase can become relative between paths

The sign is invisible to measurements on a single isolated spin when it multiplies the whole state. That does not make it irrelevant in every coherent controlled experiment. Consider two orthogonal path labels a and b, with a spin prepared identically on both paths. Rotate only the spin in path b by 2π.

$$
\frac{|a\rangle+|b\rangle}{\sqrt2}\otimes\psi
\longmapsto
\frac{|a\rangle-|b\rangle}{\sqrt2}\otimes\psi.
$$

The final spin factor is still ψ, but the two path superpositions are orthogonal. Recombining the paths in their sum/difference basis can distinguish them. The phase is now relative between paths, rather than common to the complete preparation.

This is an analytic extension of the single-spin example, not an extra operation supplied by its numerical engine. The current family has no path register, controlled gate or interference apparatus. Composite systems and entanglement are studied later; this example only identifies why a statement about local probabilities needs its scope.

## 9. Compare the spinor with a three-dimensional rotation

The same active U rotates the Bloch vector by the ordinary right-handed Rodrigues formula. The two calculations use different mathematical objects and can be compared independently.

$$
\mathbf r'=\mathbf r\cos\phi+(\mathbf a\times\mathbf r)\sin\phi
+\mathbf a(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf r)(1-\cos\phi),\qquad
r'_i=(U\psi)^\dagger\sigma_i(U\psi).
$$

For a +z rotation of the +x preparation, this gives r′=(cosφ,sinφ,0); at π/2 it points along +y. Compare that sign with the previous +x rotation of +z. Specifying the axis, initial state and active convention prevents ambiguous statements that an arrow simply rotates “clockwise.”

If both the state direction r and measurement direction n are rotated together, their dot product stays fixed. That is a covariance statement. Rotating the state alone while holding n fixed is a different experiment and can change probabilities. A passive coordinate change also transforms the coordinates of both state and observable; it must not be mixed with an active state rotation.

## 10. Exact turns, intervals and numerical evidence

The preparation uses seventeen bounded integer fields for the raw state, two directions, a signed rational turn, a positive ℏ and the sample count. Raw vectors must be nonzero. Each direction defines P=vv†/(v†v), so its Bloch unit vector and the initial spin algebra are exact rationals. Spin means have action units and variances have squared-action units.

For rotation, rational turns are reduced modulo 2 exactly. Half-turn landmarks are recognized by an exact integer condition. General angles use a proved π enclosure and directed trigonometric intervals, with 768-bit work arithmetic and outward 512-bit reported observations. An angle near 1 turn is not rounded to 1 turn for classification. Output norms are checked rather than repaired by renormalization.

A rounded fidelity of 1 near a return does not prove an exact return. A zero-containing residual interval is also different from a structurally exact zero. An unresolved observation can retain null rather than acquiring an invented drawing coordinate. The independent reference uses general high-precision matrix exponentials and symbolic eigenspaces; it does not reuse the production turn reduction or π routine.

## 11. Exercises with explanations

**1. What are the x and z laws for the +z preparation?**

**Explanation.** The z result is +ℏ/2 with probability 1. The x outcomes ±ℏ/2 each have probability 1/2. This does not assign both component values simultaneously to one classical object.

**2. Why is a component's second moment ℏ²/4 rather than 3ℏ²/4?**

**Explanation.** Both squared component outcomes are ℏ²/4. The larger number sums three different squared operators and belongs to the Casimir observable S².

**3. Where does a positive π/2 X rotation send e₀?**

**Explanation.** To (1,−i)/√2, with Bloch vector (0,−1,0). The negative y sign follows from the chosen active, right-handed convention.

**4. Can one local spin histogram detect −ψ after a 2π rotation?**

**Explanation.** No. All local Born probabilities and ray fidelity are unchanged. The representative squared difference is 4, while a coherent path-dependent sign can have a relative-phase effect in an extended experiment.

**5. If ℏ doubles at the same declared geometric angle, what changes?**

**Explanation.** The spin means double and variances quadruple. The rotation matrix and amplitudes stay fixed because ℏ cancels between the generator S and the denominator in the exponential.

**6. May a turn value 999999/1000000 be classified as exactly 1?**

**Explanation.** No. It is a distinct rational angle. Nearby probabilities or fidelity may be visually indistinguishable after rounding, but exact turn classification and interval certificates must retain the distinction.
