## Separate a physical system from its coordinates

Changing the units used to describe a motion should preserve its physical predictions when every quantity is transformed consistently. A value expressed in centimeters has a different numerical coordinate from the same value expressed in meters. Keeping the old coefficient number while changing its unit generally changes the physical system. This lesson makes that distinction visible with a trajectory, a force, and a kinetic energy calculation.

The laboratory prescribes one-dimensional constant acceleration with initial position zero. Inputs are physical mass in kilograms, initial velocity in meters per second, acceleration in meters per second squared, and a query time in seconds. Three additional integer controls choose the new length, mass, and time units. These controls change the representation; the blue reference motion continues to use the original physical inputs.

## Derive the numerical transformation rule

Let a new base unit be a positive scale factor times the old base unit. If a quantity has dimension-exponent vector d, the derived unit changes by the product of the corresponding scale powers. Keeping the quantity fixed therefore requires the inverse change in its numerical value:

$$
u'_i=\alpha_i u_i,\qquad
\{Q\}_{u'}=\frac{\{Q\}_u}{\prod_i\alpha_i^{d_i}}.
$$

For clarity the laboratory uses decimal scale factors with integer exponents between minus three and three. It does not claim that this restricted grid exhausts possible unit systems. These conversions are multiplicative; an affine temperature-coordinate change with an origin offset is outside this experiment. Measurement uncertainty is another question and is not generated by an exact decimal unit conversion.

## Transform velocity, acceleration, and time together

Denote the length, mass, and time scales by alpha_L, alpha_M, and alpha_T. Then the numerical initial velocity, acceleration, mass, and query time become

$$
v'_0=v_0\frac{\alpha_T}{\alpha_L},\qquad
a'=a\frac{\alpha_T^2}{\alpha_L},\qquad
m'=\frac{m}{\alpha_M},\qquad
t'=\frac{t}{\alpha_T}.
$$

The squared time scale in acceleration is essential. Converting a meter-based acceleration into a centimeter-based acceleration cannot be done by changing only a label. Converting the time unit also changes the numerical time at which the trajectory must be evaluated. Using the same time number after changing seconds to milliseconds would compare different physical instants.

## Prove trajectory covariance algebraically

The original constant-acceleration trajectory and its new-coordinate form are

$$
x(t)=v_0t+\frac12at^2,\qquad
x'(t')=v'_0t'+\frac12a't'^2.
$$

Substituting the transformation rules and multiplying the new position coordinate by its length unit factor cancels every scale:

$$
\alpha_L x'(t/\alpha_T)=v_0t+\frac12at^2=x(t).
$$

This is a coordinate-consistency identity under the stated model. It does not establish that a real moving object has constant acceleration. Drag, a changing force, measurement error, or an unmodeled constraint may make the model empirically inadequate while the coordinate identity remains correct.

The blue trajectory is calculated in the original coordinates. The orange dashed trajectory is calculated using transformed coefficients and transformed time, then reconstructed in meters. They coincide. Both are plotted against physical time in seconds, avoiding a misleading comparison of two different time coordinates on one unlabeled horizontal axis.

## Follow the centimeter and gram example

Use mass two kilograms, initial velocity one meter per second, acceleration two meters per second squared, and query time two seconds. The physical results are position six meters, final velocity five meters per second, force four newtons, and kinetic energy twenty-five joules.

Now set the new length unit to 0.01 meters and the new mass unit to 0.001 kilograms, leaving the time unit unchanged. The new numerical mass is 2000, initial velocity 100, acceleration 200, and position 600. The force unit is 0.00001 newtons, so the force number is 400000. The energy unit is 0.0000001 joules, so the energy number is 250000000. Large numbers in a new representation do not imply a stronger physical force or greater physical energy.

## Include milliseconds and inspect another cancellation

Set the time unit to 0.001 seconds as well. The same query instant now has time coordinate 2000. Initial velocity becomes 0.1 and acceleration becomes 0.0002 in the new coordinates. Substitution gives position coordinate 600 again, which reconstructs to six meters.

The force and energy conversion factors also change:

$$
F'=F\frac{\alpha_T^2}{\alpha_M\alpha_L},\qquad
E'=E\frac{\alpha_T^2}{\alpha_M\alpha_L^2}.
$$

The new force number is 0.4 and the new energy number is 250. Multiplying them by their derived unit sizes returns four newtons and twenty-five joules. This example shows why converting only length, while leaving mass or time coefficients untouched, can introduce errors that differ across physical quantities.

## Understand the green counterfactual

The green curve deliberately retains the old numerical velocity and acceleration in the new units. It still evaluates at the transformed time corresponding to each physical instant and converts the resulting position back to meters. Thus it describes the physical function

$$
x_{\mathrm{retained}}(t)=\alpha_L\left(v_0\frac{t}{\alpha_T}
+\frac12a\left(\frac{t}{\alpha_T}\right)^2\right).
$$

For centimeter and gram units with unchanged seconds, the query position becomes 0.06 meters. Including milliseconds makes it 40020 meters. These are different motions created by inconsistent coefficient retention, not a failure of coherent unit conversion. For extreme scales the shared physical axis can make the blue and orange curves look flat beside the large green trajectory; the numerical table retains their values.

At query time zero all three positions are zero. That isolated agreement does not establish equality of the trajectories. Identity scales also make the counterfactual coincide with the reference, and stationary motion can coincide under many unit choices. The interface reports a match at the query only, without turning it into a global curve-equality certificate.

## Distinguish exact arithmetic from the physical assumption

Supported physical inputs are integers, and unit factors are integer powers of ten. The implementation can therefore carry the queried conversions as reduced rational numbers. It reconstructs position, velocity, force, and energy and checks equality with exact integer cross products. Decimal readouts and plotted coordinates remain numerical representations of those results.

Mass does not affect this prescribed trajectory because acceleration is an independent input. It does affect the force required to realize that acceleration and the kinetic energy at each instant. If the physical force rather than acceleration were fixed, changing mass would require a different model relationship. Read the controlled quantities before interpreting a sensitivity experiment.

## Research extension: Transform the metric of a physics-informed loss

### A coordinate-consistent simulator can still train inconsistently

Suppose a dimensionless parameter theta predicts a length of theta meters and a speed of theta meters per second. Observations are one meter and three meters per second. Divide the two errors by reference scales of one meter and one meter per second. In these normalized coordinates, the residual vector is (theta minus one, theta minus three). The toy model cannot fit both observations exactly; that deliberate mismatch makes the weighting choice observable.

Consider a positive-definite residual metric W. Under an invertible linear change of residual coordinates S, demand equality of the complete objective for every theta, not just agreement at one fitted value:

$$
L(\theta)=r(\theta)^TWr(\theta),\qquad r'=Sr,\qquad
W'=S^{-T}WS^{-1},\qquad r'^TW'r'=r^TWr.
$$

Substitution cancels S and its transpose. Thus objective values and minimizer sets agree. If S is fixed independently of theta and r is differentiable, gradients with respect to the same theta agree as well. This does not assert identical floating-point optimizer trajectories if parameters, preconditioners, or stopping tolerances are also changed.

### Derive a unit-dependent fitted parameter

For diagonal weights, differentiation gives a weighted average of the two incompatible targets. Initially both weights are one, so the optimum is two. Express only the length error in centimeters while leaving the speed error in meters per second. Numerically S is diag(100,1). Here we change the numerical residual coordinates; if the physical reference scales are converted together with the errors before normalization, the normalized residuals already stay fixed. If the code retains unit weights, it multiplies the first squared residual by 10000. The new optimum moves almost to one:

$$
\widehat\theta={w_1+3w_2\over w_1+w_2},\qquad
\widehat\theta_{\rm naive}={10000+3\over10000+1},\qquad
W'_{\rm coherent}=\operatorname{diag}(10^{-4},1).
$$

The diagram divides each curve by its own positive leading quadratic coefficient and subtracts its minimum to compare minimizer locations. It does not suggest that the raw losses have equal magnitude. The coherent loss equals the original loss before this display normalization too.

### Relate the metric to measurement uncertainty

When a residual is modeled as a centered Gaussian vector with a fixed positive-definite covariance Sigma, its negative log likelihood has quadratic term one half r-transpose Sigma-inverse r. A coordinate change gives Sigma'=S Sigma S-transpose, so the inverse covariance transforms by the same rule as W. The log determinant changes by a constant if S is fixed and Sigma is independent of theta; it does not alter the optimizer in that setting. If covariance parameters are learned, the determinant term must be retained. Singular covariances require explicit support constraints and cannot be handled by casually taking an ordinary inverse.

Physics residuals need justified reference scales or uncertainty models of their own. Choosing weights to improve optimization is a separate modeling decision and should be declared. Unit covariance establishes consistency of a chosen objective; it cannot prove that these two observations, the simulator, or their uncertainty assumptions are physically adequate.

### Worked research question

**Question:** Does standardizing the network input guarantee unit-independent training? **Answer:** No. Outputs, derivatives in the governing equations, coefficients, residual scales, and the metric all matter. For fixed reference scales, the code proves exact rational equality for many parameter values, compares the two analytic minima, and verifies gradient equality. It is a standalone example rather than an additional control in the trajectory laboratory.

```python
from fractions import Fraction as F
def loss(t, scales, weights):
    return sum(w*(s*(t-y))**2 for s,w,y in zip(scales,weights,[F(1),F(3)]))
def grad(t, scales, weights):
    return 2*sum(w*s*s*(t-y) for s,w,y in zip(scales,weights,[F(1),F(3)]))
original = ([F(1),F(1)],[F(1),F(1)])
coherent = ([F(100),F(1)],[F(1,10000),F(1)])
naive = ([F(100),F(1)],[F(1),F(1)])
for n in range(-20,41):
    t = F(n,10)
    assert loss(t,*original) == loss(t,*coherent)
    assert grad(t,*original) == grad(t,*coherent)
for name,args in [('original',original),('coherent',coherent),('naive',naive)]:
    scales,weights=args
    effective=[s*s*w for s,w in zip(scales,weights)]
    optimum=(effective[0]+3*effective[1])/sum(effective)
    assert grad(optimum,*args)==0
    print(name,'optimum',optimum,'loss',loss(optimum,*args))
assert loss(F(2),*original) < loss(F(1),*original)
assert loss(F(2),*naive) > loss(F(1),*naive)
```

## Exercises with worked solutions

1. **Exercise:** Change only the mass input from one to five with velocity and acceleration fixed. **Solution:** The trajectory remains identical; force and kinetic energy multiply by five. This follows from the prescribed-acceleration design.
2. **Exercise:** In centimeter-second coordinates, what is the new number for acceleration two meters per second squared? **Solution:** Two divided by 0.01 equals 200. The physical acceleration is unchanged.
3. **Exercise:** Why is the millisecond time coordinate 2000 rather than two? **Solution:** Each new time unit is one thousandth of a second. The same physical duration therefore contains 2000 such units.
4. **Exercise:** Does matching all four exact reconstruction flags prove the model fits experimental data? **Solution:** No. The flags prove supported arithmetic identities for a stated model. Empirical fit requires observations and a justified comparison.
5. **Exercise:** Can the green curve match the query at zero but differ later? **Solution:** Yes. Every trajectory has initial position zero here, while later coefficients may represent different physical velocities and accelerations.
6. **Exercise:** Why is Celsius excluded from the multiplicative rule? **Solution:** A temperature coordinate with an origin shift needs an affine conversion. A scale-only formula would omit that offset.

## Source and AI connection

The separation of quantity values, numerical values, and units is described in the [BIPM SI Brochure](https://www.bipm.org/documents/20126/41483022/SI-Brochure-9-EN.pdf). The trajectory substitutions and rational conversion examples are derived explicitly above. In physics-informed AI, consistent scaling also matters for input coordinates, derivatives, physical coefficients, and loss terms. Converting a dataset without transforming the model or its residual weights can change the optimization problem even when the underlying observations describe the same physical experiment.
