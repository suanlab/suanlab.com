## Ask what the theorem actually guarantees

A symmetry is a statement about a family of configurations and velocities. Conservation is a statement about a quantity evaluated along a physical solution. Noether's theorem connects these statements through the action. It does not say that every quantity which happens to be constant on one curve identifies a symmetry of the whole model.

This lesson makes the missing conditions visible. We choose exact polynomial candidate paths, calculate their Newton residuals, and compare a corrected charge with the variation of the Lagrangian. Some candidates solve the equations; others deliberately do not. All structural decisions use rational polynomial coefficients, not a plot that merely looks flat. The model does not integrate an ODE.

The three main questions are: Is the chosen transformation a symmetry of the action up to an endpoint term? Does this entire candidate satisfy the equations of motion? Is the corrected charge constant on this entire candidate? These are separate tests.

## Fix the model and its physical units

Write position as $q=(x,y)$ in meters and velocity as $v=(\dot x,\dot y)$ in m/s. The mass $m$ is positive. Stiffnesses $k_x,k_y$ have units N/m and constant external forces $F_x,F_y$ have units N. Our mechanical Lagrangian is

$$
L_0=\frac m2(\dot x^2+\dot y^2)-\frac{k_xx^2+k_yy^2}{2}+F_xx+F_yy.
$$

The potential is $U=(k_xx^2+k_yy^2)/2-F_xx-F_yy$, so the physical force is $(F_x-k_xx,F_y-k_yy)$. A positive constant force accelerates toward positive position. It is not a spring coefficient.

The controls use integer mass and stiffness values. Force and gauge codes are divided by two. The query code is divided by twenty to give physical time in seconds, from zero to two. Curve samples occur at 1/40 s intervals, while query times occur at 1/20 s intervals. Samples draw the curves; exact polynomials determine the whole-path classifications.

## Normalize the transformation parameter

Use a dimensionless infinitesimal parameter $\epsilon$ and write $\delta q=\epsilon\eta(q)$. The three selectable generators are

$$
\eta_x=(\ell_*,0),\qquad \eta_y=(0,\ell_*),\qquad
\eta_R=(-y,x),\qquad \ell_*=1\ \mathrm m.
$$

Every component of a generator therefore has units m. Every displayed charge has units Js. For translation, $Q/\ell_*$ is a momentum; for rotation, the charge is the usual planar angular momentum. Their numerical values can coincide when the reference length is one, but their interpretations still differ. A reference unit is not permission to erase units.

For rotation, $\dot\eta_R=(-\dot y,\dot x)$. Transform both position and velocity. Rotating position while leaving velocity fixed does not implement this point symmetry. The finite rotation preserves distances; the tangent displacement $\epsilon\eta_R$ describes its first-order change, not an exact finite rotation.

## Allow an endpoint term in the action

A useful symmetry need not leave the Lagrangian itself unchanged. If its first variation satisfies $\delta L=\dot B$, the action changes by $\epsilon[B]_{t_0}^{t_1}$. The corrected charge is

$$
Q=p\cdot\eta-B,\qquad p=\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}.
$$

Here $B$ has units Js and $\dot B$ has units J. This is often called a quasi-symmetry or a variational symmetry up to a total derivative. An endpoint contribution can change the numerical action and still preserve the Euler-Lagrange equations.

There is a subtle example already in this model. A constant force gives $\delta_x L_0=F_x\ell_*$. That is the derivative of $B_0=F_x\ell_*t$. Translation remains a quasi-symmetry when $k_x=0$, even if $F_x\ne0$. Its corrected charge is $\ell_*(m\dot x-F_xt)$, not simply the mechanical momentum. Calling this translation completely broken would miss the endpoint term.

## Change the representative without changing the force

Add a total derivative generated by $G=\gamma tx$, with $\gamma$ in N:

$$
L_\gamma=L_0+\frac{dG}{dt}=L_0+\gamma x+\gamma t\dot x,
\qquad p_\gamma=(m\dot x+\gamma t,m\dot y).
$$

The extra terms cancel in the Euler-Lagrange equation. For a chosen spatial generator, the new endpoint contribution is $\delta G=\gamma t\eta_x$. The full boundary term is

$$
B=B_0+\gamma t\eta_x,
$$

where $B_0=F_x\ell_*t$ for x translation, $F_y\ell_*t$ for y translation, and zero for rotation. Consequently the gauge contribution cancels from $p_\gamma\cdot\eta-B$. The corrected charge is independent of $\gamma$, although canonical momentum, the naive canonical charge, and strict invariance of the displayed Lagrangian can change.

This addition of an endpoint function is not a demonstration of Noether's second theorem. The symmetry parameters here are global parameters; we are not introducing an arbitrary local gauge parameter into the dynamics.

## Derive the identity before imposing dynamics

Define the Newton residual with a fixed sign convention:

$$
R=m\ddot q+Kq-F,\qquad
D=\delta L_\gamma-\dot B.
$$

Thus $R$ has units N and $D$ has units J. Apply the product rule to $p_\gamma\cdot\eta$ and use $R=\dot p_\gamma-\partial_qL_\gamma$. This gives the exact identity

$$
\boxed{\dot Q=D+R\cdot\eta.}
$$

This equation holds on every sufficiently smooth candidate path. No equation of motion was assumed in deriving it. Conservation follows when the chosen transformation has $D\equiv0$ as a function of independent states and the candidate satisfies $R\equiv0$. Then $\dot Q=0$.

Conversely, $\dot Q=0$ along one curve can result from cancellation between nonzero terms. A zero residual at the query time can also coexist with a nonzero residual at other times. Inspect the coefficients, not just a cursor reading.

## Classify the full transformation

For the specified boundary terms, the global defects are

$$
D_x=-k_xx\ell_*,\qquad D_y=-k_yy\ell_*,
$$

$$
D_R=(k_x-k_y)xy+F_yx-F_xy.
$$

Therefore x translation is a global quasi-symmetry exactly when $k_x=0$; y translation requires $k_y=0$. Rotation requires $k_x=k_y$ and $F_x=F_y=0$. This is an exact coefficient test on the full configuration space.

Strict invariance is a different column. For x translation, $L_0$ is strictly invariant only if $k_x=F_x=0$. The displayed $L_\gamma$ is strictly invariant if $k_x=0$ and $F_x+\gamma=0$. In that last case the force can be nonzero: a time-dependent canonical momentum accounts for the difference. For rotation, a nonzero $\gamma$ generally makes the representative non-invariant even when the corrected action symmetry survives.

Defect coefficients multiply different physical monomials. Do not add their numerical magnitudes as though they shared one unit. The code exposes the basis and units separately and only tests whether every coefficient is exactly zero.

## Choose a candidate and test its entire path

The six position presets are $(1+t,1-t)$, $(1+t^2/2,1+t)$, $(1,1)$, $(1+t,2+2t)$, $(1+t,0)$, and $(0,0)$. Coefficients carry the SI units required by each power of physical time. These are prescribed paths, not trajectories generated automatically from the force controls.

The same path can be physical for one model and nonphysical for another. The accelerated first coordinate in the second preset solves Newton's equation for $m=1$ kg, $k_x=0$, $F_x=1$ N. It fails that equation for a free particle. The constant origin can solve a spring system with no external force while failing a system with a nonzero constant force.

The full dynamics flag tests whether both residual polynomials vanish identically. The full charge flag tests the derivative polynomial. Changing query time never changes these classifications or the complete curves.

As a positive rotational example, use unit mass, zero stiffness and force, and the first path $(1+t,1-t)$. This is a physical free motion with nonzero angular charge $Q=-2$ Js. Its rotational defect and Newton residual both vanish, so the full theorem applies. The nonzero constant distinguishes this example from a stationary origin where every displayed charge could vanish trivially.

## Work through a force-driven translation

Take $m=1$ kg, both stiffnesses zero, $F_x=1$ N, $F_y=0$, and $q=(1+t^2/2,1+t)$. Select x translation and $\gamma=1$ N. This path satisfies both equations of motion.

At one second, $x=1.5$ m and $\dot x=1$ m/s. The displayed canonical x momentum is 2 kg m/s. Multiplying by the reference length gives a naive charge of 2 Js. The force boundary is 1 Js and the gauge boundary is another 1 Js. The corrected charge is zero.

The Lagrangian variation and boundary rate are both 2 J, while $D=0$ and $R=0$. The naive charge increases at 2 J; the corrected charge stays zero. Change $\gamma$ to zero or to -1 N. Newton's equation and corrected charge do not change. At -1 N, the displayed Lagrangian even becomes independent of x, showing how cyclic-coordinate language depends on the chosen representative.

## See a symmetry on a nonphysical path

Take $m=1$ kg, $k_x=k_y=1$ N/m, no constant forces and no gauge term. Keep $q=(1+t^2/2,1+t)$, but choose rotation. The Lagrangian is rotationally symmetric, yet this polynomial does not solve the spring equations.

The corrected angular momentum is $Q=1-t-t^2/2$ Js. At one second, $Q=-1/2$ Js and $\dot Q=-2$ J. The global defect is zero. The residual contraction is $R\cdot\eta=-2$ J, so the identity still holds. Noether's theorem has not failed; one of its premises is absent.

Over two seconds, the charge changes from 1 Js to -3 Js. The integrated defect contributes zero and the integrated residual contraction contributes -4 Js. An endpoint-only check which ignores the interior residual would obscure the cause.

## See a conserved selected path without global rotation

Set $k_x=0$, $k_y=1$ N/m, no external forces, and choose $q=(1+t,0)$. This is a physical solution: free motion in x and zero displacement in the y spring. Its angular momentum is zero for all time.

Nevertheless, the full rotational defect is $D_R=-xy$, which is not the zero function. It vanishes on this particular path because $y=0$. The global rotation flag is false, the dynamics flag is true, and the selected charge flag is true. These three facts coexist without contradiction.

There is an even stronger warning. At the constant candidate $q=(1,1)$ with $k_x=2$, $k_y=1$ and zero force, $Q$ stays zero although $D_R=1$ J and $R\cdot\eta=-1$ J. The candidate is nonphysical and the transformation is not a global symmetry. Constancy alone establishes neither premise.

## Use the balance as a research diagnostic

Integrating the off-shell identity gives

$$
Q(T)-Q(0)=\int_0^T D\,dt+\int_0^T R\cdot\eta\,dt.
$$

This separates a model's explicit symmetry defect from a candidate's dynamics error. In a learned mechanics model, a small conservation error could arise from cancellation rather than from a correct force law. Report both terms, the interval and the generator normalization.

A useful bound is $|\Delta Q|\le\int|D|dt+\int\|R\|\|\eta\|dt$. Each residual component is in N and each generator component is in m, so these norms are dimensionally consistent. A small residual does not give a uniform charge bound if the generator grows without control. A position rotation generator grows with distance from the origin.

An invariant neural-network loss is not automatically a mechanical action, and an optimization iterate is not automatically an Euler-Lagrange trajectory. To use Noether's theorem in AI research, specify the action, continuous transformation, boundary conditions and evolution law. Field currents, local gauge identities and higher-derivative actions require additional treatment beyond this finite-dimensional lesson.

## Exercises with explained solutions

1. **Uniform force.** In the force-driven translation example, find the charge at two seconds when $\gamma=0$.

   **Solution:** Velocity is 2 m/s, so the canonical charge is 2 Js. The force boundary is $F_x\ell_*t=2$ Js. Their difference is zero. The nonzero force changes mechanical momentum while the improved translation charge remains constant.

2. **Representative dependence.** Keep the same force and set $\gamma=-1$ N. Is the force now absent because x is cyclic?

   **Solution:** No. The terms proportional to x cancel in $L_\gamma=mv^2/2-t\dot x$ with the appropriate SI coefficients. Its canonical x momentum is $m\dot x-t$, not the mechanical momentum. Its conservation still implies $m\ddot x=1$ N.

3. **A symmetric off-shell path.** Compute the two-second charge change for the isotropic spring candidate above.

   **Solution:** $Q(0)=1$ Js and $Q(2)=1-2-2=-3$ Js, giving -4 Js. The global defect vanishes. Integrating $R\cdot\eta=-1-t$ J from zero to two seconds gives -4 Js and explains the complete change.

4. **Discrete symmetry.** If $F=0$ and $k_x\ne k_y$, both coordinates can be reflected through the origin without changing $L_0$. Does this establish a rotational Noether charge?

   **Solution:** No. The reflection is a discrete transformation, while the selected rotation is a continuous family. Its infinitesimal defect is $(k_x-k_y)xy$, generally nonzero. A discrete invariance does not supply the missing continuous rotational invariance.

5. **A misleading zero derivative.** At $q=(1,1)$, $k_x=2$, $k_y=1$, zero force and zero velocity, verify the balance for rotation.

   **Solution:** Acceleration is zero on this prescribed constant path. Thus $R=(2,1)$ N and $\eta=(-1,1)$ m. Their contraction is -1 J, while $D=1$ J. The charge rate is zero because the two contributions cancel, even though neither global symmetry nor physical dynamics holds.

6. **A residual certificate.** Suppose $D=0$, $\|R\|\le0.01$ N and $\|\eta\|\le2$ m for a ten-second interval. Bound charge drift.

   **Solution:** Integrate the product bound: $|\Delta Q|\le10\times0.01\times2=0.2$ Js. This bounds drift of the selected normalized charge; it does not prove the residual is zero or establish a missing global symmetry assumption.
