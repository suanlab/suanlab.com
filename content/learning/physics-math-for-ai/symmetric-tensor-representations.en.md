## Two indices create a different representation

A vector transforms by v'=gv. A rank-two tensor has an action on both indices. In orthonormal Euclidean coordinates, this symmetric-tensor example uses the active action T'=gTgᵀ. For a quadratic form it ensures (gv)ᵀT'(gv)=vᵀTv. A passive coordinate change would use a different convention and should not be mixed into the same formula without tracking inverse maps and index types.

The controls specify an arbitrary real symmetric matrix T=[[p,q],[q,r]]. It need not be positive semidefinite, so not every setting is a covariance matrix. Its quadratic form is px²+2qxy+ry²; the off-diagonal coefficient contributes twice.

## Separate scalar and traceless parts

Every symmetric two-by-two tensor has a unique decomposition

$$
T=sI+\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&-a\end{pmatrix},\qquad
s=\frac{p+r}{2},\quad a=\frac{p-r}{2},\quad b=q.
$$

The trace part s is invariant under orthogonal conjugation. Directly multiplying RθTRθᵀ gives a double-angle action on the traceless pair:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}a'\\b'\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}\cos2\theta&-\sin2\theta\\
\sin2\theta&\cos2\theta\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}.
$$

A horizontal reflection first sends b to −b, then the rotation applies. The implementation compares the actual tensor matrix product with that predicted coefficient law. Tiny residual differences are reported as floating-point discrepancies rather than being used to alter the transformation rule.

## Read the coefficient diagram correctly

Blue and orange vectors lie in the (a,b) coefficient plane. They are not physical position vectors. For T=diag(2,0), s=1 and (a,b)=(1,0). A physical rotation by π/4 produces tensor [[1,1],[1,1]], whose pair is (0,1): a quarter turn in coefficient space. A physical π/2 rotation gives diag(0,2), with pair (−1,0).

Trace, determinant and eigenvalues remain fixed. Eigenvalues are s±√(a²+b²), making the PSD condition s≥√(a²+b²). For T=I the traceless pair vanishes and rotating the tensor changes nothing.

## Irreducibility depends on the group

Under continuous SO(2), the nonzero-weight double-angle plane is a real irreducible representation. Restrict to C4 generated by physical π/2 rotations: that plane now sees only −I as its generator. Every line is invariant, so the restriction becomes two copies of the alternating one-dimensional character. This differs from the irreducible single-angle C4 plane in the earlier unit.

## From two-index products to scalar, vector and higher-order blocks

The full tensor product contains more than symmetric tensors. In three dimensions every real matrix splits uniquely into scalar identity, antisymmetric and symmetric traceless parts:

$$
T=\frac{\operatorname{tr}T}{3}I+\frac{T-T^T}{2}
+\left(\frac{T+T^T}{2}-\frac{\operatorname{tr}T}{3}I\right).
$$

These Frobenius-orthogonal blocks have dimensions 1,3,5. Conjugation by an orthogonal g preserves each block. For the antisymmetric part write A=hat(a). Under SO(3), a transforms as a vector; under O(3) it is an axial vector with law a'=det(g)ga. The extra determinant is why reflections must be handled explicitly when mixing vector and tensor channels. This is the familiar 3⊗3 decomposition under proper rotations, with parity specified separately for reflections.

The five-dimensional block is irreducible under SO(3). To see this, take a nonzero tensor in an invariant subspace and diagonalize it by a proper rotation. Differences of its signed-permutation conjugates produce a nonzero diagonal difference; permutations then span all traceless diagonal matrices. A 45-degree rotation of a diagonal difference produces an off-diagonal symmetric basis tensor. Permuting axes yields all three off-diagonal basis tensors, so the invariant subspace contains all five dimensions.

In two dimensions the analogous antisymmetric line is spanned by J. It is trivial under rotations but changes sign under reflections, whereas the symmetric trace line is invariant under both. The lab covers the three-dimensional space of symmetric 2×2 tensors; the code below is a separate 3×3 extension.

| Three-dimensional block | Dimension | Transformation under SO(3) |
|---|---:|---|
| Scalar identity | 1 | Scalar |
| Antisymmetric | 3 | Axial vector, indistinguishable from a vector for proper rotations |
| Symmetric traceless | 5 | Rank-two irreducible block |

Use exact fractions to check reconstruction, orthogonality and the axial reflection law. Positivity is an additional constraint for covariance tensors; it is not implied by a representation decomposition.

## Reproduce the calculation in Python

```python
from fractions import Fraction as F
T=[[F(v) for v in row] for row in [[1,2,0],[0,-1,3],[4,0,2]]]
scalar=sum(T[i][i] for i in range(3))/3
C=[[scalar*int(i==j) for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]
A=[[(T[i][j]-T[j][i])/2 for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]
S=[[(T[i][j]+T[j][i])/2-C[i][j] for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]
assert [[C[i][j]+A[i][j]+S[i][j] for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]==T
def inner(X,Y): return sum(X[i][j]*Y[i][j] for i in range(3) for j in range(3))
assert inner(C,A)==inner(C,S)==inner(A,S)==0
axial=[A[2][1],A[0][2],A[1][0]]
# Reflect z: g=diag(1,1,-1), det(g)=-1.
signs=[1,1,-1]; reflected=[[signs[i]*A[i][j]*signs[j] for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]
axial_after=[reflected[2][1],reflected[0][2],reflected[1][0]]
assert axial_after==[-signs[i]*axial[i] for i in range(3)]
print('block squared norms:',inner(C,C),inner(A,A),inner(S,S))
print('axial before/after:',axial,axial_after)
```

## Connection to scientific AI

Covariances, stresses, diffusion tensors and higher-order neural features require transformation laws appropriate to their indices. Treating all channels as unrelated scalars discards those laws. Specify tensor order, symmetry, metric conventions and positivity constraints before designing equivariant layers or interpreting learned tensor fields.
